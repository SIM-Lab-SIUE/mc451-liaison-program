Codebook Walkthrough

Codebook Walkthrough: Building Your Measurement Tool

Think of a codebook as a set of instructions for reading your data. If you handed your codebook to a stranger, they should be able to look at the same 100 articles you looked at and come up with the same codes. That consistency is what makes content analysis scientific rather than just opinion.

This page walks you through building a codebook from scratch, using a worked example so you can see what a finished product looks like.

What Goes in a Codebook?

A codebook has two parts:

  1. A variable list — Every piece of information you plan to record for each unit of analysis (each article, tweet, news segment, etc.)
  2. Coding rules — Plain English instructions for how to assign values to each variable, including how to handle edge cases

The Example Study

For this walkthrough, we’ll build a codebook for a hypothetical study:

Research Question: “How do major U.S. newspapers frame COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy — as a medical issue, a political issue, or a social/cultural issue — in front-page coverage from 2021?”

Unit of analysis: Each front-page newspaper article that mentions vaccine hesitancy.

Sources: New York Times, Washington Post, Wall Street Journal.

Now let’s build the codebook.

Step 1: Start With Identification Variables

Every codebook needs variables that identify which article you coded. These aren’t part of your analysis — they’re bookkeeping.

Variable Name What It Captures Type Allowed Values
article_id Unique ID you assign Text Any (e.g., NYT-001, WP-042)
coder_initials Who coded this article Text Your initials (e.g., “JS”)
date_coded When you coded it Date YYYY-MM-DD
publication Which newspaper Categorical NYT, WP, WSJ
pub_date Article publication date Date YYYY-MM-DD
headline Article headline Text Copy exactly from source

Coding rule: Copy the headline exactly. Do not paraphrase. If the headline is cut off in your database, note “[truncated]” at the end.

Step 2: Add Your Main Analysis Variables

These are the variables tied directly to your research question. For our example study, the main variable is frame type.

Variable Name What It Captures Type Allowed Values
primary_frame How hesitancy is primarily explained Categorical medical, political, social, mixed, none
hesitancy_tone How hesitancy is evaluated Categorical sympathetic, critical, neutral
source_type_dominant Most prominently quoted source Categorical expert, official, layperson, no_source
mentions_race Does article explicitly mention racial disparities? Boolean yes, no

Step 3: Write the Coding Rules

This is the most important part. Rules need to be specific enough that two coders make the same decision on the same article.

Variable: primary_frame

Definition: The dominant explanation given in the article for why people are hesitant about vaccines.

Coding rule: Read the full article. Ask yourself: “According to this article, what is the main reason people are hesitant?” Assign one of the following codes:

Code Definition Example language to look for
medical Hesitancy is framed as concern about safety, side effects, or lack of data “side effects,” “not enough research,” “long-term effects unknown”
political Hesitancy is framed as tied to political identity, distrust of government, or policy disputes “partisan divide,” “Republican counties,” “government mandate,” “freedom”
social Hesitancy is framed as driven by cultural distrust, misinformation spread, or community norms “misinformation,” “social media rumors,” “historical distrust,” “community leaders”
mixed Two or more frames receive roughly equal emphasis (neither dominates) Use when no single frame accounts for more than 60% of explanation
none Article mentions hesitancy but does not explain it Article simply states hesitancy exists without analysis

Decision rule for mixed: If you genuinely cannot determine which frame is primary after reading the full article, code mixed. If one frame is clearly more prominent even if another is mentioned, code the primary frame.

Variable: hesitancy_tone

Definition: The overall evaluative stance toward people who are vaccine hesitant.

Code Definition
sympathetic Hesitant individuals are portrayed with understanding; their concerns are treated as legitimate or understandable
critical Hesitant individuals are portrayed negatively; hesitancy is framed as dangerous, irrational, or selfish
neutral No clear positive or negative stance; article describes hesitancy without evaluation

Important: Code the tone toward hesitant people, not toward vaccines themselves.

Variable: source_type_dominant

Definition: The type of source most prominently quoted or cited in the article.

Code Definition Examples
expert Scientists, doctors, public health researchers “Dr. Smith, an epidemiologist at Johns Hopkins…”
official Government officials, politicians, agency spokespersons “CDC Director…,” “Sen. Jones said…”
layperson Ordinary citizens, patients, community members “Maria, 34, a teacher from Ohio…”
no_source No human sources quoted Data-only articles, editorials with no attribution

Decision rule: If two source types are equally prominent, use the one that appears first in the article.

Variable: mentions_race

Definition: Does the article explicitly discuss racial or ethnic disparities in vaccine hesitancy or vaccine access?

  • Code yes only if the article uses explicit racial or ethnic categories (e.g., Black Americans, Latino communities, white rural voters)
  • Code no if race is not mentioned, or if only vague references like “underserved communities” appear without racial specificity

Step 4: Handle Edge Cases in Your Pilot

When you first apply your codebook to 10–20 real articles, you’ll hit situations you didn’t anticipate. Document every one.

Format for each decision log entry:

VARIABLE: primary_frame
ARTICLE: NYT-007
PROBLEM: Article discusses both anti-government sentiment AND concerns about mRNA technology in equal depth
DECISION: Coded "mixed" — both political and medical frames are clearly developed across multiple paragraphs
RULE UPDATE: Added to "mixed" definition: "two frames receiving roughly equal paragraph count"

These entries become part of your codebook. They show that you thought carefully about hard cases rather than guessing.

What the Finished Codebook Looks Like

Here’s a sample completed row from the example study:

Variable Value Notes
article_id NYT-003
coder_initials JS
date_coded 2024-10-15
publication NYT
pub_date 2021-04-22
headline “Vaccine Hesitancy Persists in Rural Areas Despite Outreach”
primary_frame social Focus on community trust and misinformation networks
hesitancy_tone sympathetic Framed as understandable given history of medical mistrust
source_type_dominant layperson Three community members quoted; one CDC official mentioned briefly
mentions_race no No explicit racial categories used

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Making categories too broad. If every article gets coded “mixed” because every article mentions multiple things, your categories aren’t distinct enough. Sharpen the definitions.

Not writing decision rules. Variable names alone aren’t enough. Write out exactly what counts and what doesn’t.

Skipping the pilot test. You will encounter something you didn’t expect. The pilot test is where you find those cases before you’ve coded 200 articles.

Changing definitions mid-project. If you realize a definition needs to change after you’ve started coding, go back and re-code everything you already coded. Using different definitions at different points invalidates your results.
