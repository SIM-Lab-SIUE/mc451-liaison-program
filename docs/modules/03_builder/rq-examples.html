Research Question Examples: Strong, Weak, and Revised

One of the hardest parts of content analysis is writing a research question that is both specific enough to answer and broad enough to matter. This page shows you what good research questions look like — and how to fix ones that aren’t quite there yet.

The Two Core Templates

Most content analysis research questions follow one of two patterns:

Framing / descriptive: > “How does [Source X] frame [Topic Y]?”

Relational / comparative: > “What is the relationship between [Variable A] and [Variable B]?”

Both templates work because they name specific, measurable things. The moment you can say exactly what you’d look for in your data, you have a viable research question.

Strong Research Question Examples

Example 1 — Framing Analysis

“How do U.S. national newspapers frame climate change as an economic issue versus an environmental issue in front-page coverage from 2018–2023?”

NoteWhy this works
  • Source is specific: “U.S. national newspapers” (you’d define which ones — NYT, WaPo, WSJ, etc.)
  • Topic is specific: climate change, and two named frames (economic vs. environmental)
  • Measurable: You can code each article as economic, environmental, both, or neither
  • Bounded: “front-page coverage” and “2018–2023” tell you exactly what to collect
  • Answerable: After coding 100+ articles, you can calculate the percentage for each frame

Example 2 — Relational Analysis

“What is the relationship between source type (government official vs. scientist vs. activist) and the uncertainty language used in news coverage of the COVID-19 vaccine?”

NoteWhy this works
  • Variable A is specific: source type — and three distinct categories are named
  • Variable B is specific: uncertainty language — which you’d define in your codebook (e.g., words like “may,” “could,” “preliminary”)
  • Measurable: You can code both variables for every article
  • Yields a real finding: You’ll be able to say whether scientist quotes use more/less hedging than government quotes

Example 3 — Platform Comparison

“How does Twitter/X coverage of the 2024 election differ from newspaper coverage in terms of emotional tone (positive, negative, neutral)?”

NoteWhy this works
  • Comparison is built in: Twitter vs. newspapers (the platform is a variable)
  • Emotional tone is measurable: three discrete categories that you can code consistently
  • Event is bounded: the 2024 election gives you a clear time frame
  • Comparative design: lets you run a simple frequency comparison between the two platforms

Weak Research Questions — and How to Fix Them

Weak Example 1 — Too Vague

❌ “How do media outlets cover immigration?”

Problems: - “Media outlets” — which ones? All of them? That’s thousands of sources. - “Cover” — in what way? Tone? Volume? Framing? Sources quoted? - “Immigration” — what aspect? Border policy? Asylum seekers? Undocumented workers?

✅ Revised: “How do Fox News and MSNBC differ in the sources they quote (government officials, immigrants, advocates, researchers) in primetime immigration coverage from January–June 2024?”

What improved: - Outlets are named (Fox, MSNBC) - “Cover” is replaced with a specific measurable thing (sources quoted, with categories defined) - Immigration is narrowed to a specific time window - The comparison is built into the question itself

Weak Example 2 — Not Measurable

❌ “Is social media harmful to teenagers?”

Problems: - “Harmful” is not a variable — it’s a conclusion. You can’t code an article as “harmful.” - This question belongs in a health/psychology study, not a content analysis - There’s no source or text to analyze

✅ Revised: “How do parenting magazines frame social media use among teenagers — as primarily harmful, primarily beneficial, or mixed — in articles published between 2020 and 2024?”

What improved: - The source is defined (parenting magazines) - The frame categories are explicit (harmful / beneficial / mixed) - It’s now a content analysis question, not a social science experiment - The time frame is bounded

Weak Example 3 — Too Broad to Code

❌ “What is the relationship between race and media coverage?”

Problems: - “Race” needs to be operationalized — race of whom? The subject? The reporter? The source quoted? - “Media coverage” — which outlets? What time period? What topic? - This would take a team of researchers years to answer

✅ Revised: “What is the relationship between the race of a crime suspect (as reported in the article) and the use of criminalizing language in local TV news crime reports in St. Louis from 2022–2023?”

What improved: - Race is operationalized as “race of the suspect as reported in the article” - “Criminalizing language” is defined (you’d spell this out in your codebook) - Outlet type (local TV news), geography (St. Louis), and time frame (2022–2023) are all specified - This is still a big project, but it’s concrete enough to actually do
